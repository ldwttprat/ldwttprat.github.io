My story took shape on the high plains of central Wyoming, a landscape famous for its desolate expanses, fierce winds, and conservative culture. From early on I felt more outside than in, and books were the way out. Stories of the fearless, independent pioneer women who had walked the same rugged ground delighted me, as did stories from much farther away. Bedside, I kept paper folded maps of the world and studied them constantly.

A Japanese aunt living across the country sparked my interest in Asia early on, and thanks to her I started reading stories about and by women in Japan and the lands they walked. I left Wyoming for college with a year of Japanese behind me, another rare and lucky encounter, and went straight into Japanese and Asian history, literature, philosophy, politics, and religion. Appetite kept me there as much as aptitude. Oh, the pleasure of not understanding something yet!

I went into religious studies because the gap between religious texts, with their lofty ideals, and the messy realities of how people actually live was too intriguing to leave alone. The environment and gender were my angles of approach, and the path since, shaped by those early encounters and nurtured by a series of especially fine mentors, has led me through an array of spaces.

Leaving the university in 2022 was a bigger turn than any move on the map. I went into industry research, and the path led to Paris, where I’m based with my family. Multidimensionality is my practice: I lead global research on emerging technologies, translate professionally between Japanese and English, and move between academia and industry as what I like to call “an interpretive scientist.” The distance I went into religious studies to examine is the distance I examine now—lofty ideals written down on one side, the messy realities of people living with them on the other. Only the texts have changed: designs, specs, and models in place of scripture.

Teaching is another story, and an important one, mostly belonging to the university years. Between 2007 and 2022 I taught at seven universities in five countries on three continents. One type of teaching I especially love happens far from any classroom: field courses. I’ve led students through Japan’s temples, shrines, and museums, and along the ancient pilgrimage trails of Yoshino and Ōmine.

Becoming and being a mother—internationally, no less!—has handed down more lessons about transmission and reception than any text ever could.

And then we have all the rest.
I’ll end here with just a few of my favorite things:

road trips.

dinner parties.

walks in nature.

dance sessions with my favorite DJ (my husband)

and his two superfans (our daughters).
